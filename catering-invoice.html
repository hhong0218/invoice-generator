Catering Invoice Template

Catering jobs bundle food, staff, rentals, and travel into a single event — and clients usually pay a deposit up front and the balance after. This guide shows how to structure a clear catering invoice that accounts for all of it, and you can create one free in your browser.

How caterers price an invoice

Most catering invoices are built from one of these pricing models, and larger events often combine several:

  • Per head. The most common structure — a set price per guest for a chosen menu. Put the per-plate price in the unit column and the guest count in the quantity column.
  • Per platter or per tray. Common for drop-off and buffet catering, where food is sold by the tray rather than by the plate.
  • Package or per-event. A flat price for a defined package (menu, service, and setup) — simplest for weddings and corporate functions with a fixed scope.
  • Add-on lines. Bar service, dessert stations, dietary or allergen-friendly options, and premium menu upgrades listed separately so the base price stays readable.

Deposits and the final balance

Catering almost always involves a deposit, because you commit to buying ingredients and booking staff before the event happens. There are two clean ways to show this on an invoice. You can issue a deposit invoice first (for example 50% to reserve the date), then a final invoice for the remaining balance after the event. Or, on a single invoice, list the full total and add a “deposit paid” line as a negative amount so the balance due reflects only what’s outstanding. Whichever you choose, state the deposit terms clearly — amount, due date, and refund policy — so there’s no dispute if the event changes.

Staffing, rentals, and travel

The food is only part of a catered event. Servers, bartenders, and kitchen staff are often billed by the hour per person, so list the role, the hourly rate, and the hours. Rented items — linens, tables, chafing dishes, glassware, or a tent — can be itemized or rolled into a package, but itemizing builds trust on larger jobs. If the venue is far, a travel or mileage line is normal. Gratuity or a service charge, where you apply one, should be shown as its own clearly labeled line rather than folded silently into the totals.

Sales tax on catering

Prepared food and catering services are taxable in many places, and rules on what portion is taxable — food, service, gratuity — vary by state and country. Apply the correct rate for where the event is held, show the tax as its own line, and keep a copy of every invoice for your records. This tool isn’t tax advice, so if you’re unsure how catering is taxed in your area, check with your local authority or an accountant.

Look professional, get paid on time

A tidy, itemized invoice reassures a client who has just trusted you with their wedding or company event. Make sure each invoice carries a unique number, the event date, your business and the client’s details, itemized lines, any deposit already paid, the subtotal, tax, and the balance due. Frequently asked questions

How do I show a deposit on a catering invoice?

Either issue a separate deposit invoice up front and a final invoice for the balance, or list the full total on one invoice and add a “deposit paid” line as a negative amount so the balance due shows only what’s still owed. State the deposit amount, due date, and refund policy clearly either way.

Should I charge per head or a flat package price?

Per-head pricing suits events with a firm guest count and a chosen menu, while a flat package works well for weddings and corporate functions with a fixed scope. Many caterers quote per head and add package upgrades as separate lines.

How do I bill for servers and rentals?

List staff by role with an hourly rate and hours, and itemize rented items like linens, tables, and glassware — or roll them into a package. Itemizing tends to build more trust on larger jobs.

Is catering subject to sales tax?

Prepared food and catering are taxable in many places, but the rules on food, service, and gratuity vary by location. Apply the correct rate for where the event is held and show tax as its own line; check locally if you’re unsure.
