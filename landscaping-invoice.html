Landscaping Invoice Template

Landscaping billing lives in two worlds at once: one-off design and build projects, and steady recurring maintenance like mowing and seasonal clean-ups. Each is invoiced differently, and doing both well keeps cash flowing across the season. This guide shows how, and you can generate an invoice free in your browser and download a clean PDF.

Two ways landscapers get paid

Almost every landscaping business runs a mix of the two, and the invoice should suit each:

  • Project work. Patios, planting, turf, fencing, and full garden makeovers — usually quoted as a fixed price or in stages, with materials a big part of the total.
  • Recurring maintenance. Mowing, hedge trimming, weeding, and leaf clearance on a weekly, biweekly, or monthly cycle — billed as a flat visit fee or a monthly rate.

Keeping these on separate invoices, or at least separate line groups, makes each customer’s bill easy to read and your own bookkeeping cleaner.

Invoicing recurring maintenance

Maintenance is the backbone of a landscaping income because it recurs. The simplest approach is a flat fee per visit; the tidiest for the customer is a fixed monthly rate that averages the season out, so they pay the same whether you mowed four times or five. Whichever you use, keep the description consistent (“Monthly lawn and garden maintenance — June”) and issue on the same date each cycle so the customer can reconcile it at a glance. Consistent, predictable invoices are the ones that get paid on autopilot.

Billing project work and materials

For build projects, materials — turf, plants, paving, aggregate, timber — often outweigh labor, so itemize them. List significant materials individually at your supply price with quantities (for example “Sandstone paving, 24 m²”), and put crew labor on its own line as hours or days. For larger jobs, stage the billing: a deposit to order materials, a progress payment partway, and the balance on completion. This protects your cash while plants and stone are sitting in your van rather than in the customer’s garden.

Crew labor, equipment, and disposal

If you run a crew, you can bill labor as a combined crew rate per hour or day rather than listing each person — simpler for the customer and it protects your margins. Don’t forget the costs that are easy to leave off: skip hire or green-waste disposal, and any specialist equipment hire (a mini-digger, a stump grinder). List these as their own lines so a big job’s real costs are covered and visible, not quietly eating your labor rate.

Payment terms and seasonality

For maintenance, monthly invoicing with net–7 or net–14 terms keeps the cycle tight. For projects, deposits and staged payments matter because landscaping is seasonal — a slow winter is easier when spring and summer jobs were billed promptly and on stage. Put accepted payment methods on every invoice, along with a unique number, issue and due dates, itemized lines, subtotal, tax, and the total due. Frequently asked questions

How should I invoice recurring lawn maintenance?

Use a flat per-visit fee or a fixed monthly rate that averages the season out. Keep the description consistent and issue on the same date each cycle so the customer can reconcile it easily and pay on autopilot.

Should I take a deposit for a landscaping project?

Yes for larger build jobs. Stage the billing: a deposit to order materials, a progress payment partway through, and the balance on completion. This protects your cash while materials sit in your van.

How do I bill for crew labor?

Bill a combined crew rate per hour or day rather than listing each person individually. It is simpler for the customer to read and protects your margins.

What about disposal and equipment hire?

List green-waste disposal or skip hire and any specialist equipment hire, such as a mini-digger or stump grinder, as their own line items so a big job’s real costs are covered rather than eating into your labor rate.
