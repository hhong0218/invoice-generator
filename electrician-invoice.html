Electrician Invoice Template

Electrical work mixes skilled labor, marked-up materials, permits, and sometimes an emergency call-out — all on one job. A well-structured invoice separates those clearly so the customer sees exactly what they are paying for and approves it without a call back. What an electrician invoice should show

Whether you run domestic rewires, panel upgrades, or commercial fit-outs, the same building blocks apply. A clear electrical invoice itemizes:

  • Labor. Your hourly or day rate multiplied by time on site. For a fixed-price job, enter it as a single labor line instead.
  • Materials and parts. Cable, breakers, outlets, fixtures, conduit — listed at your supply price, which usually includes a markup over trade cost.
  • Call-out or trip fee. A flat charge to cover travel and the first period on site, common for diagnostic and emergency work.
  • Permits and inspection fees. Any charges you paid to the local authority, passed through as their own line.

Call-out fees and emergency work

Emergency and after-hours electrical work is priced differently from planned jobs, and the invoice should make that obvious. Put the call-out fee on its own line, and if you charge a premium rate outside normal hours, label it so — for example “Evening emergency rate” — rather than burying it in a higher hourly figure. Customers accept surcharges far more readily when they are stated plainly and agreed before the work, so quote the call-out and rate up front and simply reflect it on the invoice.

Billing labor and materials separately

Splitting labor from materials is the single most useful habit for an electrical invoice. It lets the customer see the value of your skilled time distinct from the cost of parts, and it makes any warranty or dispute far easier to resolve. List each significant material on its own line with a quantity and unit price; smaller consumables can be grouped as a single “sundries” line. If you mark up materials, that markup is already built into your supply price — you do not need to show trade cost.

Permits, inspections, and pass-through costs

On regulated work you may pull a permit or pay for a third-party inspection. Pass these through at cost as their own line items so they are not mistaken for labor. Keep the receipts — some customers, especially landlords and commercial clients, will want the permit reference and inspection certificate number recorded, and noting them on the invoice saves a follow-up email.

Payment terms and deposits

For small repairs, “due on receipt” or net–7 keeps cash moving. For larger installs, a deposit up front (to cover material orders) with the balance on completion is standard. State accepted payment methods — card, bank transfer, or a payment link — directly on the invoice, and make sure every invoice carries a unique number, issue and due dates, your license or registration number if required locally, itemized lines, subtotal, tax, and the total due. Frequently asked questions

Should I list materials and labor separately?

Yes. Separate lines for labor and for each significant material make the invoice transparent, show the value of your skilled time, and make warranty or dispute questions far easier to resolve. Group small consumables as a single sundries line.

How do I invoice a call-out or emergency fee?

Put the call-out fee on its own line and label any after-hours premium clearly, such as “Evening emergency rate.” Agree the fee before the work; customers accept surcharges readily when they are stated plainly rather than hidden in the hourly rate.

Should I take a deposit for larger installations?

For bigger jobs, yes — a deposit up front covers your material orders, with the balance due on completion. For small repairs, due-on-receipt or net–7 terms usually work fine.

How do I handle permit and inspection fees?

Pass them through at cost as their own line items, separate from labor, and note the permit reference or inspection number if the customer needs it for their records.
