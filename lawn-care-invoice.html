Lawn Care Invoice Template

Lawn care is a route business: dozens of properties, small tickets, and the same services repeating week after week. The money is made — or lost — in how efficiently you can turn a day’s route into paid invoices. Here is a billing structure that scales with your customer list; you can generate each invoice free in your browser and download a clean PDF.

What a lawn care invoice should show

Across a season, most lawn accounts are billed from this menu:

  • Mowing visit. One line per cut — mow, edge, string-trim, and blow off hard surfaces — at your per-visit price for that property size.
  • Treatment applications. Fertilization and weed-control steps, numbered so the customer can follow the program (“Application 3 of 6 — summer fertilizer”).
  • Seasonal services. Aeration, overseeding, and dethatching — higher-ticket once-a-year lines.
  • Shrub and hedge trimming. Billed per visit or per hour when it is not part of the standing agreement.
  • Spring and fall cleanup. Leaf removal and bed cleanup, priced per job with hauling included or listed separately.
  • Mulch, installed. Priced per cubic yard including delivery and spreading, with the yardage shown.

Per visit or flat monthly?

Per-visit billing is transparent — the customer pays for exactly the cuts they received — but it swings with the weather and multiplies your paperwork. A flat monthly rate spreads the season’s expected visits into equal payments, smooths your revenue, and cuts your invoicing to twelve predictable bills per customer per year. Growing operations tend to migrate from per-visit to flat monthly as the route fills. Whichever you choose, put the visit dates covered on each invoice so there is never a “did you even come?” conversation.

Rain days and skipped visits

Weather is the one guarantee in this business, so your invoice policy should already have an answer. On per-visit billing, a rained-out week is simply not billed — the invoice lists the visits that happened. On flat monthly, the rate already averages missed and extra cuts, and it helps to say so on the invoice footer (“Monthly rate reflects seasonal visit average”). If a customer asks to skip a scheduled cut on short notice, many operators bill a reduced hold fee if the route stop was reserved — whatever your rule is, print it, because a policy on the invoice is a policy the customer accepted.

Treatment programs need numbered steps

If you run a fertilization and weed-control program, invoice each application as a numbered step of the whole (“Application 4 of 6”), with the service date and what was applied described in plain terms. Customers stay enrolled when they can see the program progressing, and your records stay clean for any product or licensing documentation your state requires. Where treatments are prepaid for the season, show each visit as a zero-balance line against the prepayment so the customer sees the value being delivered.

Batch your billing like you batch your route

The route is efficient because the stops are grouped; do the same with invoices. Bill every account on the same day each month, keep service descriptions identical from month to month, and number invoices sequentially so nothing goes missing. Include a payment link or card option — small recurring bills are exactly what people expect to pay online — and the usual essentials: unique invoice number, issue and due dates, itemized lines, subtotal, tax, and total due. Frequently asked questions

Should I invoice per visit or monthly?

Per-visit is transparent but paperwork-heavy and weather-dependent; a flat monthly rate averages the season into equal payments and cuts your invoicing workload dramatically. Many operators move to flat monthly as their route grows. Either way, list the visit dates covered on each invoice.

How do I handle a rained-out visit on the invoice?

On per-visit billing, simply do not bill it — the invoice lists completed visits only. On a flat monthly rate, the price already averages weather across the season; a footer note saying so prevents confusion in a light month.

How should I bill mulch and materials?

Price mulch per cubic yard installed — delivery and spreading included — and show the yardage on the line. For other materials like seed or soil, list them at your supply price separate from the labor line.

How do I invoice a prepaid treatment program?

Show each application as a numbered step with a zero balance drawn against the prepayment. The customer sees every visit delivered, and your season’s records reconcile themselves.
