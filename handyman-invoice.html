Handyman Invoice Template

A handyman’s working day is a string of small, unrelated jobs — a sticking door here, a ceiling fan there, a fence panel after lunch. The invoicing challenge is the opposite of the big trades: not one large bill, but making a punch list of little tasks read clearly on a single page. Here is how, and you can build the invoice free in your browser and download a clean PDF.

What a handyman invoice should show

Most handyman bills are assembled from these pieces:

  • Labor with a minimum. Your hourly rate, with a stated minimum — many handymen bill a one- or two-hour minimum per visit so short calls still cover the trip.
  • A line per task. “Replace bathroom faucet,” “Patch and paint hallway drywall,” “Rehang closet door” — each job on the punch list gets its own line.
  • Materials at supply price. The faucet, the hinges, the tube of caulk — billed with receipts available on request.
  • Trip or travel fee. A flat charge for getting to the property, if you charge one, shown openly rather than padded into hours.
  • Material pickup time. The run to the hardware store, billed either as time or as a flat pickup fee (more below).
  • Haul-away. Removing the old faucet, the broken door, the debris — a small line that is pure loss if you forget it.

Minimums and trip fees, stated plainly

Nobody stays in business driving forty minutes to bill a quarter hour. A per-visit minimum or a flat trip fee fixes that — but it belongs on the invoice as what it is. A line reading “Service call minimum — 1 hr” is accepted without comment; the same money disguised as inflated task hours breeds suspicion. Tell customers the minimum when they book, and the invoice simply confirms what they already agreed to.

Itemizing a punch list without the mess

When one visit covers six tasks, resist both extremes: a single vague “handyman services” line (invites disputes), and a forensic log of every screw (unreadable). The middle path is one line per task with a short plain-language description and either its hours or a flat amount. Customers scan the list, recognize each job they asked for, and pay. It also builds your paper trail: when the customer calls next month about the faucet, your invoice shows exactly what was done and when.

Materials: receipts, markup, and the store run

For small jobs the simplest materials policy is pass-through at the store price with receipts available, plus your time for the pickup run — either as billed minutes or a flat “materials pickup” fee. Some handymen instead apply a modest markup and absorb the shopping time; both are legitimate, but pick one, be consistent, and answer honestly if asked. Group genuinely small consumables — screws, caulk, sandpaper — into one “job supplies” line rather than listing them individually.

Get paid before you leave

Handyman work is one trade where same-day payment is realistic: the customer is usually home, the amounts are modest, and the work is visibly done. Put “due on receipt” on the invoice, accept a card or payment link on the spot, and hand over (or email) the finished PDF before you pull out of the driveway. Frequently asked questions

Should I charge a minimum for small jobs?

Yes — a one- or two-hour minimum per visit is common and fair, since travel and setup cost you the same whether the job takes ten minutes or two hours. State it when the customer books and show it plainly on the invoice as a service call minimum.

Can I bill for time spent picking up materials?

Yes, and you should — either as billed time or as a flat materials-pickup fee. The alternative is a consistent small markup on materials that absorbs the shopping time. Either approach works if you apply it consistently and can explain it when asked.

Do I need to itemize every small task?

One line per task with a short description is the sweet spot. A single vague line invites disputes; listing every screw is unreadable. Group tiny consumables into one job-supplies line.

How do I get paid the same day?

Set due-on-receipt terms, bring a card reader or payment link, and hand the customer the finished invoice before you leave. For repeat customers with monthly punch lists, a single end-of-month invoice also works well.
