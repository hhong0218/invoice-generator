What a painting invoice should show

Interior repaint or full exterior, the invoice draws from the same lines:

  • Surface preparation. Patching and skim work, sanding, caulking, masking, and floor protection — itemized, not hidden.
  • Paint and materials. Each product with the number of gallons (“Interior eggshell, 4 gal”), plus primer, tape, and sundries.
  • Labor by area. Priced per room, per square foot, or hourly — matching how you quoted.
  • Trim, doors, and ceilings. Detail work billed as separate lines, since brushwork takes far longer per square foot than rolling walls.
  • Extra coats and color changes. Added coverage beyond the quote, billed as a change-order line.
  • Exterior extras. Power washing, scraping and priming bare wood, and any lift or tall-ladder work.

Make the prep visible

When a customer compares your bid to one 30% cheaper, the difference is almost always preparation. Listing “Patch and sand walls,” “Caulk trim gaps,” and “Mask and protect floors” as their own lines shows where the money goes and quietly explains why the cheap job will peel. The invoice is your last and most-read piece of marketing — let it show the craft.

By the room, the square foot, or the hour?

All three conventions are common. Per-room pricing is easiest for homeowners to follow; per-square-foot suits large or commercial jobs where areas are measured anyway; hourly fits small touch-up visits. Whichever you quote in, invoice in the same unit — a job quoted per room but invoiced in hours reads like a bait-and-switch even when the total matches. Keep ceilings and trim as separate lines within each room so the detail work is priced honestly.

Who supplies the paint?

Either model works, but the invoice should say which one it is. If you supply, list each product with gallons at your price — which fairly includes your contractor discount as margin. If the customer buys their own paint, note “paint supplied by customer” on the invoice and be clear beforehand that coverage problems from their product choice may mean extra billed coats. That single sentence, agreed early, prevents the most common painting dispute.

Deposits, color changes, and touch-ups

For multi-day jobs a deposit before you order paint is normal, with the balance on walkthrough. Mid-job color changes are real work — repriming, extra coats — and belong on the invoice as a change-order line agreed when the customer changes their mind, not discovered at the end. Note your touch-up policy (for example, minor touch-ups within a stated window after completion) so the walkthrough has a clean finish line. Frequently asked questions

Should prep work be listed separately from painting?

Yes. Patching, sanding, caulking, and masking are where the hours and the quality live. Itemizing them justifies your price against cheaper bids and documents what the customer actually received.

What if the customer supplies the paint?

Note “paint supplied by customer” on the invoice and agree up front that coverage problems from their product choice may require extra billed coats. If you supply, list each product and gallon count at your price.

How do I bill a mid-job color change?

As a change-order line, priced and agreed when the change is requested — it typically means repriming or additional coats. Never absorb it silently or surprise the customer with it on the final bill.

Should I charge for an extra coat?

If the quote specified two coats and the color or surface needs a third, that is extra work — bill it as its own line. Flag it as soon as you see the coverage issue so the customer can decide before you proceed.
