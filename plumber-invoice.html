Plumbing jobs range from a ten-minute washer swap to a full bathroom install, and the invoice has to flex with them — labor, parts, a call-out charge, and sometimes an emergency premium. What to put on a plumbing invoice

Most plumbing invoices, from a leaking tap to a boiler swap, are built from the same lines:

  • Labor. Time on site at your hourly rate, or a fixed price for a defined job such as fitting a toilet.
  • Parts and materials. Fittings, pipe, valves, a new unit — each at your supply price, listed with a quantity.
  • Call-out charge. A flat fee covering travel and the first stretch of diagnostic time.
  • Emergency or out-of-hours rate. A premium for nights, weekends, and urgent leaks, shown clearly as its own line or labeled rate.

Call-out charges and emergency rates

Plumbing is one of the trades customers call in a panic — a burst pipe at midnight is worth a premium, and the invoice should reflect it honestly. Show the call-out charge separately from labor, and if an urgent job runs at an out-of-hours rate, name it so the customer sees why the figure is higher. The golden rule is to agree the call-out fee and any emergency rate before you start; a surcharge that was quoted up front and simply confirmed on the invoice almost never triggers a dispute.

Fixed-price jobs vs. time and materials

For common, predictable jobs — swapping a tap, fitting a standard toilet — a fixed price is cleaner and reassures the customer there is no meter running. Enter it as a single labor line with the job described. For diagnostic work, leak-chasing, or open-ended repairs, bill time and materials: your hourly rate against hours worked, plus each part used. Deciding which model fits before you quote, and reflecting that on the invoice, avoids awkward end-of-job conversations.

Parts, markup, and warranties

List significant parts individually at your supply price, which already includes any markup over trade cost — you do not show what you paid the merchant. Grouping small bits (washers, sealant, clips) as one “sundries” line keeps the invoice tidy. If a part or your workmanship carries a guarantee, a short note on the invoice (“New valve carries a 12-month manufacturer warranty”) is good practice and heads off later questions.

Payment terms for plumbers

For emergency and small repair work, “due on receipt” or card on completion is normal — you are on site, so collect then. For larger installs, take a deposit to cover the unit and materials and invoice the balance on completion. Every invoice needs a unique number, issue and due dates, your and the customer’s details, itemized lines, subtotal, tax, and the total due. Frequently asked questions

How do I charge a call-out fee on an invoice?

Show the call-out charge on its own line, separate from labor, and agree it with the customer before you start. A fee that was quoted up front and simply confirmed on the invoice rarely causes a dispute.

Should I use a fixed price or time and materials?

Use a fixed price for predictable jobs like swapping a tap or fitting a standard toilet — it reassures the customer. Use time and materials for diagnostic, leak-chasing, or open-ended repairs where the scope is not clear up front.

How do I handle emergency or out-of-hours work?

Charge a premium rate and label it clearly on the invoice, such as “Out-of-hours rate,” rather than hiding it in a higher hourly figure. Agree the rate before the work so the higher total is expected.

Do I list what I paid for parts?

No — list parts at your supply price, which already includes any markup. Show each significant part with a quantity, and group small consumables as a single sundries line.
